✎ 25+ years designing residential homes

Hi, I’m David.

I’ve been designing residential architecture for over 25 years.

What I enjoy most about it is being trusted by my clients with their needs, hopes, dreams and budgets, and eventually designing a home that gives them enjoyment to live in.

There are always site constraints, planning rules, building regulations and budgets to work within. I see these as part of the puzzle — working out how all the pieces can come together to allow a design to emerge that balances what is possible and buildable, without losing sight of your needs and how you want to live.

My skill is sitting with you, explaining all these different aspects, and then getting creative together. Idea by idea, sketch by sketch, an architectural design emerges balancing all these things to create something that is uniquely yours.

01 DESIGNS

What does a good home feel like?

I’ve had the privilege of working with some prominent New Zealand architects in Auckland. One thing I noticed, time and time again, was a simplicity within their work — even when the architecture itself was beautifully sophisticated. There was a balance to it. Everything seemed to influence everything else, and somehow all those individual decisions came together to create a sense of harmony.

I really appreciated those years of drawing their designs and being involved in turning ideas and developing them into drawings and seeing the actual buildings. I was learning by being part of the process — seeing how a drawing, a detail or a material became something real.

Over the years, working on my own projects, I’ve become much more conscious of the balance I’m trying to create for my clients.

I think about light, space and the way spaces connect with one another. I think about the aesthetics and the tactile quality of materials, as well as the character of the site and its surrounding landscape. None of these things exist in isolation.

When they work together, they create something more than a collection of rooms or a beautiful object. They create a feeling. And that feeling is something the people who live in the home get to experience every day.

“It’s about finding the right balance of light, warmth and shade for the way the home will actually be lived in.”

It’s really nice to have early morning sunshine in the bedroom and kitchen, and then enjoy the warmth of the afternoon sun later in the day — particularly in winter.

I like to think about how the sun moves across the site and how the house can make the most of it. Living areas and bedrooms can take advantage of the northern light, while spaces like garages and utility areas can often work well on the cooler southern side.

Being able to open the curtains and let the morning light into the bedroom, have plenty of midday sunshine in your office, or retreat to a TV room that can be darkened are all part of designing with light in mind.

And sometimes it’s simply about having somewhere warm and sunny to sit with a cup of tea and look at the view.

An outdoor room can be a great part of this too — somewhere to enjoy the sun, find some shade, or sit outside when it’s raining lightly.

“When you walk into a room, you don’t even have to look around. You can feel its size and scale almost instantly.”

Being able to envision a space that is right for the function of each room, and how people will move from one space to another, can make a big difference to how a home feels.

Sometimes space is lost in long hallways or clumsy arrangements. Finding the right proportions and making the most of the available space within the budget is a process worth going through.

It’s about exploring and discovering what the better outcome might be.

“For me, good connections are about making a home work naturally for the way you live.”

It’s fantastic when the living space connects to the kitchen, and the kitchen in turn has easy access to the laundry. It means the practical things of everyday life can happen while still being part of family life.

When friends and family come over, people often naturally congregate around the kitchen and living areas. Connecting these spaces to an outdoor room can extend that experience — providing a place for alfresco dining, shade and enjoying the garden.

There are smaller connections too. Bedrooms can be grouped naturally around the main bathroom, a master bedroom might open onto its own private deck, or a home office can sit near the front entrance, allowing clients to visit without coming through the rest of the house.

Even the garage can be positioned to create a simple, natural connection between the road and the home.

“One of the first things I do is go to the site and get a feel for it.”

Every site has its own opportunities and challenges. I look at where the sun moves, where the wind comes from, where the views are, and where privacy might need to be considered.

I also like to hear what the owners have noticed about the site — they often know things about it that you can only learn by living there.

There might be a great view, a sunny northern aspect, an established tree or even an existing water feature that we want to make the most of.

A sloping site can offer opportunities too, perhaps allowing different levels of the house to connect with the ground in interesting ways.

Then there are the constraints: privacy, planning requirements, ground stability, drainage, access and how the building can actually be constructed.

All of these things become part of the design process. The design gradually emerges through exploring and testing ideas — making the most of what the site gives us while finding thoughtful ways to deal with what it doesn’t.

The best designs feel as though they belong where they are.

“Materials can give you a feeling just by looking at them, before you even touch them.”

Matt-stained plywood can have a tender, delicate quality, while brick veneer can feel hard and strong. White plasterboard is almost neutral, while timber panelling can bring warmth and a sense of life to a space.

Flooring, too, can completely change how a room feels underfoot and visually.

When these different textures, materials and qualities are balanced, they create a rhythm throughout the home. Moving from one space to another can feel like moving through different areas, each with its own character.

With an alteration, there is another layer to consider. Sometimes an existing home has a timeless quality that is worth keeping.

The challenge is to add what is needed for modern living while respecting what is already there. It takes thought and careful detailing to make the new sit comfortably with the old — so the original character isn’t lost, but becomes part of what makes the new home special.

“Good design is about making sure the money is spent in the right places.”

Getting the best possible design within a client’s budget has become a particular skill of mine.

After many years of designing, I can usually get a feel for where spending money will make a real difference, and where it may not.

Sometimes that means making the most of what is already there in a renovation, avoiding unnecessary structural changes and spending that money on practical items, such as kitchen and bathroom finishes and fixtures.

Other times it means simplifying construction, while putting more thought into the spaces, connections and light — things where thoughtful design can make a difference to how a home feels.

The money is going to be spent anyway. Good design is about making sure it is spent in the right places.

For me, the best outcome is when the budget, the practical requirements and the design all come together — and the homeowners get to enjoy the result every day.

02 WE WORK TOGETHER

The design becomes something we discover together.

I like designing with you, rather than taking the brief away and coming back with a finished drawing.

We can share the screen and work through ideas together. As I draw in 3D, you can see the house taking shape — the proportions, the spaces, the connections and how it sits on the site.

We can move things around, try something different and see what happens.

Plans and elevations are important, but they can be difficult to understand when you’re not used to reading architectural drawings. Working in 3D means we can look at the design together and talk about what you’re actually seeing and feeling.

We can also take an existing house or a developing design and use visual tools to explore ideas for materials, colours, trees and landscaping.

These aren’t the final architectural drawings — they’re a way of bringing an idea to life and helping us understand where it might go.

“For me, that’s one of the most enjoyable parts of designing: having an idea, drawing it, looking at it together, changing it, and seeing what emerges.”

Selected projects

A selection of residential projects exploring character, lifestyle, site, views, budget and thoughtful alteration.

01 ⌂ FRENCH STYLED COUNTRY HOME
01 — FRENCH STYLED COUNTRY HOME

Vision, character, lifestyle

Steve and Jo were looking for a lifestyle change in Matakana. They imagined a French-inspired country home — somewhere relaxed and rustic, with room for their children, space to entertain and a strong connection to the outdoors.

Their inspiration came from magazine images of exposed timber trusses, open fireplaces, brick gable ends and the character of traditional French country homes.

The challenge was to capture that feeling while creating a home that was comfortable, practical and enjoyable to live in every day.

Covered verandahs outside the bedrooms and generous outdoor living spaces helped bring the house into the landscape, while the materials and detailing created the rustic character they were looking for.

As Steve and Jo put it:

“David totally ‘got it’ — the house was exactly what we had in mind.”

They were so pleased with the result that they planned to work with me again on their next project.

02 ⌂ AUCKLAND VILLA REFURBISHMENT
02 — AUCKLAND VILLA REFURBISHMENT

Reworking what already exists

Mel and Andy’s villa had previously been divided into two homes. They wanted to bring it back together as a single family home, while working within a tight renovation budget.

Rather than adding more building, we looked at what was already there and reworked the layout.

This created an additional bedroom and improved the flow between the kitchen, dining and living spaces, with a strong connection through to the sunny backyard.

The renovation respects the character of the original villa while making the home work for modern family life.

03 ⌂ GRANDPARENTS BEACH HOUSE
03 — GRANDPARENTS BEACH HOUSE

Site, views, constraints, family

Dennis and Carol wanted a modern, low-maintenance beach house where their grown children and grandchildren could come together, while making the most of the sea views.

The steep site brought its own challenges, including geotechnical and foundation considerations, protecting large native trees and accommodating an onsite wastewater system.

The response was a two-storey, mono-pitch home that worked with the slope rather than fighting it.

Open-plan living, large windows and two separate lounges provide plenty of space for family gatherings, while keeping the house simple and easy to maintain.

The finished home sits comfortably among the trees and makes the most of its coastal setting.

As Dennis and Carol put it:

“The finished product met all our needs, with very few queries from the builders — David was the right choice for our project.”

04 ⌂ VILLA ADDITION
04 — VILLA ADDITION

Character, budget, thoughtful alteration

Sarah and Jonathan, a couple with a young family, needed more space in their Mt Eden villa — a larger family room and kitchen, a new ensuite and laundry.

They had already explored plans with another architect, but were concerned about the cost and amount of work involved.

The challenge was to find a way to give them what they needed without losing the character of the original villa.

Rather than starting again, we looked carefully at what was already there. By incorporating the existing 1920s lean-to into the new living space, adding a new bullnose verandah and paying close attention to the detailing, the new work could sit naturally alongside the original house.

The result was more space and better functionality, while retaining the character that made the villa worth keeping.
